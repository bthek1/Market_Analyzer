Here's a comprehensive breakdown of the major variables:

## Macroeconomic Factors

### Interest Rates
The single biggest lever — set by central banks (RBA in Australia, Fed in the US):
- **Rates up** → borrowing costs rise → corporate profits fall → stocks fall
- **Rates up** → bonds become more attractive → money moves out of stocks
- **Rates down** → opposite effect, cheap money flows into equities
- Markets often move *before* rate decisions based on expectations

### Inflation
```
High inflation → central bank raises rates → stocks fall
Low/controlled inflation → rates stay low → stocks supported
Deflation → economic stagnation signal → stocks fall
```

### GDP Growth
- Strong GDP → corporate revenue grows → earnings up → stocks up
- Recession (2 quarters negative GDP) → earnings fall → stocks fall
- **Leading indicators** watched before GDP prints: PMI, retail sales, employment

### Employment
- Low unemployment → consumer spending up → corporate revenue up
- US Non-Farm Payrolls (NFP) — released monthly, moves markets instantly
- Too low unemployment → wage inflation → central bank raises rates

### Currency Strength
- Strong AUD → Australian exporters earn less when converting foreign revenue
- Weak USD → US multinationals earn more from overseas
- Commodity prices (oil, gold, iron ore) priced in USD — currency moves affect them directly

## Corporate Fundamentals

### Earnings (EPS)
The most direct driver of individual stock prices:
```
Revenue growth
Operating margin
Net income
Earnings Per Share (EPS)
Forward guidance  ← often more important than the actual result
```
- A company can beat earnings but drop if guidance disappoints
- Earnings season (quarterly) drives significant individual stock volatility

### Valuation Multiples
```
P/E ratio — price relative to earnings
P/B ratio — price relative to book value
EV/EBITDA — enterprise value vs operating profit
```
- When rates rise, high P/E "growth" stocks get hit hardest (future earnings worth less)
- Value stocks (low P/E) tend to outperform in high-rate environments

### Debt Levels
- High debt + rising rates = squeezed margins, potential default risk
- Debt-to-equity ratio watched closely during rate hiking cycles

## Market Sentiment & Psychology

### Fear & Greed
Markets overshoot in both directions based on emotion:
- **VIX (Volatility Index)** — "fear gauge", spikes during sell-offs
- **Put/Call ratio** — high put buying signals fear
- Sentiment often matters more than fundamentals short-term

### Momentum
- Stocks that are rising tend to keep rising (trend following)
- Institutional money flows amplify moves in both directions

### Narrative
Markets trade on stories:
```
2020-2021 → "AI will change everything" → tech bubble
2022      → "inflation is permanent" → everything sold off
2023      → "soft landing possible" → recovery rally
```

## Geopolitical Factors

| Event | Market Effect |
|---|---|
| War / conflict | Risk-off, energy prices spike, defence stocks up |
| Trade war / tariffs | Supply chain disruption, affected sector sell-off |
| Election outcomes | Policy uncertainty, sector rotation |
| Sanctions | Commodity supply disruption |
| Central bank policy meetings | Instant volatility on surprise decisions |

## Sector-Specific Variables

### Energy
- Oil price (Brent, WTI) — drives energy sector, affects inflation broadly
- OPEC production decisions
- Transition to renewables long-term

### Technology
- Interest rates (growth stocks are rate-sensitive)
- AI/innovation cycles
- Regulation (antitrust, data privacy)

### Financials (Banks)
- Interest rate spread (borrow short, lend long)
- Credit default rates
- Regulatory capital requirements

### Materials / Mining (ASX-heavy)
- China demand — biggest buyer of iron ore, coal, copper
- Commodity cycle
- AUD/USD exchange rate

### Healthcare
- Drug approval (FDA/TGA decisions)
- Patent cliffs
- Government policy (Medicare, PBS pricing)

## Liquidity & Money Flows

### Central Bank Balance Sheet (QE/QT)
- **Quantitative Easing (QE)** — central bank buys bonds, injects money → stocks up
- **Quantitative Tightening (QT)** — reversal → liquidity drains → stocks down
- 2020-2021 Fed QE was a massive tailwind for all asset prices

### Institutional Flows
- Pension fund rebalancing (sell winners, buy losers at quarter/year end)
- ETF flows — passive investing means money flows into all stocks in an index
- Margin calls — forced selling amplifies crashes

### Retail Sentiment
- Less dominant but measurable — Reddit (WSB), social media driven moves
- Options market activity from retail can force dealer hedging (gamma squeeze)

## Technical Factors

```
Support / resistance levels    — price levels where buying/selling clusters
Moving averages (50/200 day)   — crossovers watched by algorithmic traders
Relative Strength Index (RSI)  — overbought/oversold signals
Volume                         — confirms or contradicts price moves
```
Technical analysis is self-fulfilling to a degree — enough traders watching the same levels makes them real.

## Key Relationships to Understand

```
Rates up     → growth stocks down, value stocks less affected
Inflation up → commodities up, bonds down, rate-sensitive sectors down
USD up       → emerging markets down, US multinationals down
Oil up       → energy stocks up, airlines/transport down, inflation up
China growth → iron ore up, ASX materials sector up (big deal for ASX)
Recession    → defensives (utilities, healthcare, consumer staples) outperform
```

## What Actually Moves Markets Day-to-Day

In rough order of impact:

1. **Central bank decisions / statements** — instant, large moves
2. **Inflation data** (CPI, PCE) — moves rate expectations
3. **Employment data** (NFP, unemployment rate)
4. **Earnings reports** — individual stocks, but large caps move indices
5. **Geopolitical events** — unpredictable, sudden
6. **GDP prints**
7. **Consumer confidence / PMI**
8. **Currency moves**
9. **Technical levels** — algorithmic reactions
10. **Sentiment / news flow**

The market is ultimately a **discounting mechanism** — it prices in *expectations* of the future, not current reality. A bad economy can have a rising market if things are "less bad than feared", and vice versa.
